Thomas Alva Edison:

Innovation: Light Bulb

Thomas Edison's innovations revolutionized modern life, focusing on practical applications like the incandescent light bulb, the phonograph, and the motion picture camera, but he also pioneered the industrial research lab (Menlo Park) and developed electric power systems, holding over 1,000 patents by applying systematic experimentation, teamwork, and business acumen to transform ideas into viable products, demonstrating invention as a process.


Alexander Graham Bell

Innovation: Telephone

Alexander Graham Bell's most famous invention is the telephone, patented in 1876, but his work spanned many fields, including the Photophone (transmitting sound on light), Graphophone (sound recording), Metal Detector (for President Garfield), Audiometer (hearing measurement), and significant contributions to aeronautics (kites, hydrofoils) and hydrofoils, driven by his lifelong interest in speech, hearing, and communication


The Wright Brothers

Innovation: Airplane

The Wright brothers, Orville and Wilbur Wright, invented the first successful airplane, achieving the first controlled, sustained flight of a powered, heavier-than-air aircraft on December 17, 1903, at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, with the Wright Flyer. Their invention wasn't just about power but also control, as they developed key methods like wing-warping for steering, making their aircraft practical for the first time and paving the way for modern aviation


Tim Berners Lee

Innovation: Internet

Tim Berners-Lee's key invention is the World Wide Web (WWW), developed in 1989 at CERN to help scientists share information easily, combining hypertext, the Internet, and personal computers. He created the first web browser, server, and foundational technologies like HTML (HyperText Markup Language), HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol), and URIs (Uniform Resource Identifiers), making information accessible globally.


Johannes Gutenberg

Innovation: Printing Press

A printing press is a mechanical device that transfers ink from a surface (like movable type or a plate) to paper or other media, enabling the mass production of texts and images, most famously revolutionized by Johannes Gutenberg in the 1440s with his use of movable metal type, making books affordable and spurring literacy and knowledge. Modern presses have evolved from hand-operated wooden models to fast, automated digital and offset machines, but the core principle of applying pressure to transfer ink remains.The Gutenberg Bible was the first major book printed in Europe using movable metal type, created by Johann Gutenberg in Mainz, Germany, around 1454-1455.
